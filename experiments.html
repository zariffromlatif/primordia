Experiments

Each experiment asks one question by running twin islands. Twins rise from the sea with the same terrain and the same founders, and differ in just one rule. Islands vary a lot on their own, so every experiment runs several pairs and counts how many came out the same way.

Experiments run in your browser. A run takes one to six minutes on a laptop, longer on a phone, and gives the same result every time, in every browser.

Watch one species become two

When the sea cuts an island in two after three years, one lineage often holds both halves, and sometimes it splits into two species, one on each side, as Darwin's finches did on their islands. These links open rich islands, where plants grow back twice as fast and each bite is worth twice as much, so there are about twice as many creatures to watch.

Splits are rare, so this isn't measured with twins like the experiments above. Running the scenario headless with npm run speciation, one lineage held both halves when the sea came on 13 of 20 rich islands, and became two species on 3 of them, two to four years after the flood. On islands with the usual richness it happened on 2 of 20, and on 7 of 40 more checked separately.

  • Island 1014: Ilralo frugalis in the west and Ilralo pratensis in the east
  • Island 1015: Drisa argus in the west and Mina modesta in the east
  • Island 1019: Annaqua robusta in the west and Annaqua nomada in the east

Set the speed to 40× and keep an eye on the family tree. The sea floods the middle after three years, and the split shows as a new branch, a few years later.
