Experiments

Each experiment asks one question by running twin islands. Twins rise from the sea with the same terrain and the same founders, and differ in just one rule. Islands vary a lot on their own, so every experiment runs several pairs and counts how many came out the same way.

Experiments run in your browser. A run takes a minute or two on a laptop, longer on a phone, and gives the same result every time in the same browser.

Watch one species become two

On a rich island, where plants grow back twice as fast and each bite is worth twice as much, populations are big and steady enough for one lineage to hold both halves after the sea cuts the island in two, and sometimes to split into two species, one on each side, as Darwin's finches did on their islands.

Splits are rare, so this isn't measured with twins like the experiments above. Running the scenario headless with npm run speciation, one lineage held both halves when the sea came on 16 of 20 rich islands, and became two species on 3 of them within three years of the flood. On islands with the usual richness, that happened on none of 20.

  • Island 1003: Mishidri quieta in the west and Mishidri parva in the east
  • Island 1004: Oran magna in the west and Ilsave placida in the east
  • Island 1014: Shiexdri vulgaris in the west and Shiexdri minima in the east

Set the speed to 40× and keep an eye on the family tree. The sea floods the middle after three years, and the split shows as a new branch, a few years later.
